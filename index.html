What is an Australian degree worth?

Eleven charts on graduate starting salaries — where the money is, which fields lead, and what a student trades away when they choose a discipline.

DomainHigher education & labour market outcomes
AudienceProspective and current Australian university students
DataABS · QILT Graduate Outcomes Survey 2016–2025
Section 01 — Why this matters

Choosing a degree is the biggest financial decision most Australians make in their twenties.

Tuition, three to five years out of the workforce, and the opportunity cost of not earning while studying all add up. But the payoff isn't uniform: a Pharmacy graduate starts on roughly $62,600; a Dentistry graduate starts on $103,100. That's a 65% gap between two fields that both demand five years of study.

This visualisation brings together two public datasets — the Australian Bureau of Statistics' regional income tables and the federal government's Graduate Outcomes Survey — to answer three questions a prospective student actually asks: which fields pay best, does the university matter, and what do I give up by choosing one path over another?

A note on scope. Every chart that follows is built from median starting salaries — the middle graduate in each group, not the average of extreme earners. Medians are less distorted by a handful of very high incomes, and better represent what a typical student should expect.

Section 02 — The geography of income

Income isn't spread evenly — and neither is the opportunity to earn it.

Before we look at graduates, it helps to see the wider income landscape they enter. Australia's highest earners cluster in a handful of inner-city regions; the lowest cluster in remote and regional areas. Two maps establish that context.

Where the earners are — and where inequality is worst.

Plotting both the number of earners and the Gini coefficient of each region reveals a second layer to the story. The biggest bubbles are in the capital cities — no surprise — but the darkest ones sit in the wealthiest suburbs of Sydney and Melbourne.

Sydney's Eastern Suburbs has a Gini of 0.63 — well above the national average of 0.48. That's a region where the top 1% of earners take home nearly a fifth of all income. Meanwhile Western Sydney — with a similar population of earners — sits in the palest band.

Where you study and work shapes how much you earn. But the effect is small compared to the effect of what you studied — a finding we return to in Section 04.

Section 03 — Does the university matter?

Universities in regional Australia often out-earn the sandstone Group of Eight.

The common assumption is that the prestigious, big-city universities produce the highest-paid graduates. The data suggests otherwise. Two charts explore what graduates from each Australian university actually earn, and how likely they are to land full-time work.

Every university pays postgraduates more — but by how much varies sharply.

Undergraduate salaries cluster tightly between $67k and $87k across every Australian university. Postgraduate coursework salaries, at the same institutions, span $90k to $135k — nearly twice the spread.

The University of New England, a regional campus in Armidale, has the highest undergraduate median in the country. The University of New South Wales tops the postgraduate list. Neither is a mistake: the split reflects what graduates actually do — regional nursing, teaching, and health graduates move quickly into paid roles, while metropolitan graduates are more likely to pursue further study or casual work first.

Section 04 — Which fields pay best?

The field you choose matters more than the university you attend or the year you graduate.

Salaries have risen roughly 30% across every field since 2020, and the ranking of those fields has barely moved. If you want the highest starting salary in Australia, the answer is stable and blunt: study Dentistry. If you want the lowest, the answer is equally stable: Pharmacy, Communications, or Creative Arts.

The stability is the finding. Over six years — including a pandemic, a cost-of-living crisis, and a tight graduate labour market — not a single field has moved more than three places in the ranking. Field choice locks in your starting salary more than any other factor in this dataset.

Section 05 — Beyond the headline number

Two charts complicate the story: pay gaps and diminishing returns.

The previous sections measured salaries as if all graduates in a field were identical. They aren't. Gender changes the picture, and so does the level of qualification you stop at.

A gender pay gap in every field — but not the same one.

Female graduates earn less than male graduates in nearly every field, even at the point of first employment. The gap is widest in Architecture and Communications (around 8–10%), and smallest in Nursing and Pharmacy, where the workforce is highly feminised and award wages dominate.

The story isn't one of universal disparity — it's that the gap depends on the field. This matters for students trying to interpret the "typical salary" for their discipline.

Each rung of the education ladder adds less than the one before it. A bachelor's degree adds roughly $19k over the general workforce median. A master's adds $24k more. A PhD adds only $11k on top of that — despite, typically, three to four additional years of study.

Section 06 — No field wins on everything

Salary, employment, and job-match form a triangle — and every field leans toward one corner.

The final three charts step back from single numbers. They ask: if we could measure a field's profile across three outcomes at once, which fields look balanced, and which look lopsided?

Salary and employment don't move together — you trade one for the other.

Drag the slider to see how the relationship between salary and full-time employment has shifted year by year. Very little has changed: Pharmacy and Rehabilitation sit at the top of the employment axis but bottom of the salary axis; Dentistry sits at the top of salary but mid-pack on employment.

This is one of the most useful findings in the dataset. If a student cares most about getting a job, the safest fields are the health-adjacent ones. If they care most about earning power, they should choose the high-salary fields and accept a slightly lower employment rate.

The average international graduate earns less than the average domestic graduate at the same university. In 2024 the gap was $7,000 — down from a peak of $12,000 in 2022. Both groups' salaries grew steadily; the recent narrowing likely reflects a change in the fields international students are choosing.

Conclusion

Three things a student can take from this data.

1. Field first.

Field of study moves starting salaries by $40k. University choice moves them by $20k. Geography moves them by less. If you can only optimise for one thing, optimise for the field.

2. Employment isn't automatic.

Even in the most employable fields, 5–10% of graduates are still looking for full-time work four months after finishing. In less-employable fields the figure is closer to 20%.

3. Postgraduate study has a sweet spot.

A coursework master's adds the biggest premium. A PhD adds the smallest. For most students, the return on a research degree is not financial.
