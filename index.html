How degrees affect starting salaries?

Graduate starting salaries across fields of study, universities, and regions — built from the ABS regional income tables and the QILT Graduate Outcomes Survey.

1 · Every decision matters, affecting your earning potential

The starting salary varies widely across fields of study, universities, and the level of education.

Fig 01. Average starting salary by highest qualification, 2025. Stems run from $0. Grey bands show the range from the lowest- to highest-paying fields.

Look at the grey bands. They show the massive range of salaries within each qualification. The field you choose creates a $40k swing—proving that what you study matters just as much as how long you study.

What level of education offers the best return on investment?

Every additional year of study costs time, tuition, and lost wages. So when it comes to starting salaries, how much does each level of education add? The answer is not a straight line.

The biggest leap in earning power happens at the Postgraduate coursework level. Moving from an undergraduate degree ($76k) to a postgraduate coursework degree ($101k) adds roughly $24,000 to median starting salary. For many students, this represents the "sweet spot" of higher education.

However, pushing further to a Postgraduate research degree (like a PhD) adds only $11,000 to that median. When you factor in the three to four extra years of study and the opportunity cost of not working, the financial return on a PhD is significantly lower than a Master's.

What gives the best return on investment in higher education?

The answer is postgraduate. Undergraduate salaries cluster tightly between $67k and $87k across almost every Australian university. It doesn't matter much which one you attend for your bachelor's degree; the field you study is the dominant factor.

But at the postgraduate level, the spread is nearly twice as wide ($90k to $135k). A postgraduate degree from a top-tier institution—or one with strong industry connections—can push your starting salary far above the national average. If you are going to pay for a Master's, the university you choose matters significantly more.

2 · The regional income level is uneven over Australia, but income level does not determine graduate starting salaries

Australia's highest earners cluster in a handful of inner-city regions; the lowest cluster in remote and regional areas. Two maps establish the economic context for everything that follows.

Fig 02. Median personal income by SA4 region, 2022–23 (ABS). Darker blue represents higher income.

Where are the highest earners located?

Before looking at graduates, we need to establish the economic baseline they are entering. The darkest regions—Sydney's North Shore, the WA Outback North, and the ACT—are the highest earners in the country. Conversely, regions like Wide Bay in Queensland, South-East Tasmania, and the Mid North Coast in NSW represent the lowest income levels.

This chart shows the average income level for everyone in a region, not just graduate students. But it establishes the economic context for the job market that graduates are entering.

Average income only tells half the story. For a university student, the question is: How much can they get for their starting salary? Do graduates follow this exact same geographic divide, or does the specific university they attend matter more?

The next chart explores the median starting salaries of graduates from different universities across Australia.

Do Melbourne and Sydney always pay the most?

This map plots every Australian university recorded on QILT at its real geographic location. The colour of each dot represents the median starting salary for its 2025 undergraduate cohort, from the palest pink (lowest salaries) to the darkest red (highest salaries).

The common assumption is that studying in a major city—like Sydney or Melbourne—guarantees the highest starting salary. But the data busts that myth. The highest-paying university in the country (the darkest red dot at $87,100) is the University of Southern Queensland, located in the regional town of Toowoomba. Meanwhile, some universities in major metro areas (like the lightest dot in Adelaide at $67,800) sit at the bottom of the pack.

But salary is only one half of the equation. A high-paying job is useless if you can't get one. The next map asks: if we look at employment rates instead of salaries, do these same universities still come out on top?

3 · Employability tells a different story for different universities

The common assumption is that popular, big-city universities produce the highest-paid graduates with higher employability. The data suggests otherwise.

Fig 04. Proportional symbol map of full-time employment rates by university. QILT GOS 2025.

Do the most prestigious universities offer better employment outcomes?

This map plots every Australian university at its geographic location. The size of each bubble represents its full-time employment rate—the percentage of 2025 undergraduates who found a full-time job within four months of finishing.

The common assumption is that the prestigious Group of Eight (in the major capital cities) guarantee the best employment outcomes. But the data busts that myth. Some of the largest bubbles on the map belong to regional universities, such as Charles Sturt University and James Cook University.

Why do regional universities perform so well here? These institutions often have incredibly strong ties to local health, education, and agriculture employers, creating a direct pipeline from graduation to a full-time job. Prestige doesn't always equal employability.

4 · The field you choose matters more than the university you attend

Salaries have risen roughly 30% across every field since 2020, and the ranking of those fields has barely moved. Dentistry leads. Pharmacy trails.

How has the salary landscape shifted over five years?

This slope chart tracks every single undergraduate field of study between 2020 and 2025. At first glance, the overwhelming pattern is upward movement. Every field experienced a salary increase over this period, generally rising by roughly 30%.

Notice how the lines run almost perfectly parallel. This is the crucial insight: while the whole economy inflated, the relative hierarchy of these fields never changed. A field that was at the top in 2020 is still at the top today, and those at the bottom stayed at the bottom.

Over 5 years, Dentistry started on $84,000 and jumped to $103,100. Meanwhile, Pharmacy started on just $49,600 and only reached $62,600. While Dentistry leads the pack and makes the salary gap greater, the rest of the fields are more clustered together over the 5 years.

The spread between the highest and lowest earning fields is a massive $40,000, and that gap has not narrowed in five years. Your choice of field locks in your baseline salary.

Fig 06. Slope chart. Median starting salary for all 21 undergraduate fields in 2020 vs 2025.

Fig 07. Heatmap of median undergraduate salary by field and year, 2020–2025. Fields sorted by 2025 salary.

Is the ranking of fields stable over time?

The lines in the slope chart shows a parallel growth, but this heatmap proves just how deeply entrenched those rankings are. Here, every field is tracked across six years. Darker red cells represent higher salaries, and the fields are sorted from highest-paying at the top to lowest at the bottom.

If the graduate job market were volatile, you would see the dark bands zig-zagging up and down as fields traded places. Instead, look at the horizontal bands—they barely shift.

Even through a global pandemic, a cost-of-living crisis, and massive economic shifts, the vertical hierarchy remained practically frozen. No field managed to jump more than three places in the salary rankings.

This tells us that the graduate job market is highly segmented. Your choice of field essentially locks in your starting salary bracket. Climbing from the bottom rows (like Pharmacy or Creative Arts) to the top rows (Dentistry or Medicine) is incredibly difficult, even over half a decade.

But salary isn't everything, there are other things you should consider when choosing a field, such as employment and job-match. The next section explores the trade-offs between these three outcomes.

5 · No field wins on everything at once

Which fields look balanced across multiple outcomes, and which fields lean heavily in one direction?

Can one field dominate in salary, employment, and job-match simultaneously?

Each dot is a field. Position shows where its profile leans—toward salary strength, employment strength, or job-match strength. A dot near the centre is equally strong (or weak) on all three.

Look at the extremes. Pharmacy sits at the bottom edge: it has one of the strongest employment profiles, but the weakest salary. Creative Arts leans hard toward the job-match corner, reflecting the fact that graduates feel they are using their degree, even though both salary and employment strength are low.

A field like Medicine sits towards the top: strong salary and strong job-match. But it's important to note what this triangle doesn't show—the human cost. The fields at the top of the triangle (Medicine, Dentistry) require years of additional study and significant financial investment.

The centre of the triangle is often the most deceptive. A dot in the "balanced zone" isn't necessarily winning; it often just means the field is average at everything. It lacks a standout reason to choose it.

But this chart only shows the shape of the profile; it doesn't tell us how extreme the differences are. To see the magnitude of these trade-offs, we have to look at the ranks.

Fig 08. Ternary balance plot. Undergraduate 2025.

Does a "balanced" profile mean a field is actually a winner?

The ternary plot above shows the shape of a field's profile. A dot near the centre might look "balanced," but it can also mean a field is simply average at everything. To see the real stakes, we have to look at the ranks.

This bump chart reveals the brutal trade-offs hiding behind the profiles. Pharmacy sits mid-pack in the triangle, but here you can see it is actually Rank 1 for employment and Rank 21 for salary. Conversely, Dentistry is Rank 1 for salary but only mid-tier for employment.

The rank on each axis tells the real story of what you are trading away when you choose a field. The centre of the triangle is a trap: it hides the fact that you might be sacrificing top-tier earnings for top-tier employability.

You cannot have it all. The field that gets you the highest salary is rarely the same field that gets you the highest employment rate.

Fig 09. Rank position of each field across salary, full-time employment, and job-match (low overqualification), 2025. Coloured lines highlight extreme trade-offs.

Do higher salaries come with better employment?

Rarely. Drag the slider to watch the years tick by. The dots move, but the relationship stays the same: fields like Pharmacy and Rehabilitation sit at the top of the employment axis but the bottom of the salary axis. High pay and high employment are often a trade-off, not a package deal.

Does citizenship status affect your starting salary?

Yes, but the gap is narrowing. The international–domestic salary gap widened sharply through 2022, but has been closing since. Both groups' salaries grew steadily, but the recent narrowing likely reflects a shift in the fields international students are choosing, rather than a sudden change in pay equity.

6 · Beyond the headline number: gender and qualification level

Treating all graduates in a field as identical hides two important variations. Gender changes the picture, and so does how far you take your education.

Fig 12. Male vs female median starting salary, by broad field. Undergraduate 2025. Sorted by the size of the gap.

Is the gender pay gap universal across all fields?

Female graduates earn less than male graduates in almost every single field, even at the very first step of their careers. But the size of the gap depends heavily on the discipline.

The gap is widest in Architecture and Law and paralegal studies (around 8–10%), and smallest in Agriculture and environmental studies and Social work, where the workforce is highly feminised and award wages dominate.

The story isn't one of universal disparity—it's that the gap is highly context-dependent. This is crucial for students interpreting the "typical salary" for their chosen discipline. The median salary advertised for a field might hide a significant divergence between what men and women are actually offered.

Three things a student can take from this data

1 · Interest first

There is no perfect field to study, fields that are strong on some outcome and weak on others. There is always a trade-off. The difficulty of the field is not shown in this study, fields like Dentistry with higher rank on every outcome comes with a much higher workload and longer study time. Students should prioritise interest first, and treat salary and employment as secondary considerations.

2 · Selection of University is important

The choice of university matters in employment and salary outcomes, especially at postgraduate level. Some universities have strong ties to local employers which have more employment opportunities, while some universities have higher starting salaries. Students should consider the university's location, reputation, and graduate outcomes when making their decision.

3 · Master's degrees offer the best return

Master's degrees are the only postgraduate qualification that consistently out-earn undergraduate degrees in terms of starting salaries. PhDs are the least rewarded in salary, despite the fact that it requires significant more time and financial investment.
